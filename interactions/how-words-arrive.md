# How the person's words arrive

Most of what you read was said, not written: free-flowing conversation and
storytelling, not careful testimony. The precision is whatever the person
gave. "Sometime in high school" stays sometime in high school; never sharpen
it into a year they did not say. A landmark, or a date given deliberately and
exactly, is the exception: take it as stated.

Much of it is dictated by voice-to-text, so the transcript itself can be wrong:

- misheard words and wrong homophones: "exact Incorrect" for "exactly
  correct", "there mission" for "their mission";
- mangled names: one surname spelled three ways across three stories is one
  person until something says otherwise;
- numbers run together or split: "19 to 21" arrives as "1921", "two
  thousand" as "2000".

Read for what they most plausibly meant, in context and against the rest of
the record. "Dad served his mission at 1921" is an age range, 19 to 21, not a
year before Dad was born. When you record such a reading, write the meaning
in digits ("19 to 21"). When a reading is genuinely ambiguous and it matters —
a date, an age, a name — raise it as a question rather than guessing
silently.

People misspeak, too. Something that contradicts what they said earlier is a
question to ask them, never a correction to file over their earlier words.
